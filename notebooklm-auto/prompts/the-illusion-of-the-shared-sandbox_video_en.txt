### [Monologue Script - Mingli]
When someone demands that you give them a formal definition of causality before any inquiry can begin, notice the posture behind that request.

It sounds responsible. But look at what the demand actually requires.

To define anything, you need words, premises, and logical derivation. Every syllogism you construct, and every sentence you speak, already assumes that an antecedent act will produce a consequent state. That asymmetry is causality itself.

You cannot define causality without already presupposing causality. Causality is not an object discovered inside the landscape of thought; it is the generative canvas upon which thought takes place. Demanding an external proof or a reductionist definition for the very condition of possibility forces the mind into an infinite circle.

When someone demands that definition, they aren't looking for foundational ground. They are trying to reduce an autonomous subject into a predictable object—building a neat, manageable sandbox where they can sit as an external judge.

And that points to an even deeper misconception about human communication. We comfort ourselves with the belief that when we talk, our thoughts are neatly packed into words and unpacked intact inside another consciousness.

It doesn’t happen. Minds are discrete causal centers. What we call consensus is not shared internal models—it is an operational clearing price.

In a financial market, a transaction happens not because the buyer and seller agree on the intrinsic value of an asset, but precisely because they disagree. The buyer values the stock more than cash; the seller values cash more than the stock. The transaction price is a structural artifact of mutual divergence.

Public words work the exact same way. We use words like "truth," "choice," and "causality" to clear transactions and avoid constant collisions, while hosting non-identical internal universes.

Recognizing this boundary changes how we observe the world. Words and measurements are low-dimensional readouts. They are valuable as navigational data—giving you the friction to update your own trajectory and take your next step (+1). The moment you mistake that readout for an exhaustive verdict on someone else's interiority, you mistake the shadow on the sand for the living mind.
